How the calculation works

We treat the milk as a single uniform temperature (lumped thermal mass) that loses heat exponentially toward the bath. Three thermal resistances sit in series between the milk and the cooling water.

Newton's Law of Cooling

The energy balance on the milk:

ρ · V · c_p · dT/dt = −U · A · (T − T_env)

This ODE has the closed-form solution:

T(t) = T_env + (T₀ − T_env) · exp(−t / τ)

Time to reach a target temperature

Inverting the exponential gives the formula used for every result on this page:

t* = τ · ln[ (T₀ − T_env) / (T_target − T_env) ]

Time constant τ

τ = ρ · V · c_p / (U · A)

ρ · V · c_p is the thermal mass of the milk. U · A is the total thermal conductance between milk and bath.

Overall heat-transfer coefficient U

Three resistances in series — inner convection (milk → wall), wall conduction, outer convection (wall → bath water):

1/U = 1/h_i + d/k + 1/h_o

Effective surface area A (cylindrical bottle, radius r)

A_submerge = 2V/r + 2πr² (sides + both caps) A_sitting = 2V/r + πr² (sides + top cap; bottom rests on counter) A_running = 2V/r (sides only; no bottom or top contact with flow)

With running tap water the bottle is held under the flow so neither the bottom nor the top cap contacts the stream — only the cylindrical sides are wetted. Although flowing water has a higher h_o (800 vs 300 W m⁻² K⁻¹), the net conductance U·A is about 32 % lower than a still submerged bath — making running tap the slowest water option.

Why 70 °C? ⓘ

The WHO/FAO Safe preparation, storage and handling of powdered infant formula guidelines — specifically about reconstituted powder formula (PIF), not breast milk — recommend using water at no less than 70 °C when mixing the powder (preparation step: "use water that has been boiled and cooled for no more than 30 minutes so it remains at or above 70 °C"). At that temperature Cronobacter sakazakii and Salmonella, which can contaminate dry formula powder during manufacture, are killed.

At ≈ 70 °C the first visible small bubbles appear in heating water — dissolved gases nucleating on the vessel surface, not true steam boiling.

Limitations

The lumped model assumes the milk is well-mixed (uniform temperature). In practice a small gradient exists; real cool-down times may differ by ±10–20 %. The bottle neck, teat and cap are ignored — they add thermal resistance and reduce the effective area slightly, so actual times will tend to be a little longer than shown.
